Opening story
The Opening Problem

Bwalya knew the odds. He kept betting anyway.

Knowing the right answer and doing the right thing are not the same skill. This module is about the gap between the two — and how to close it.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Opening (5 min)
Ask before revealing anything: "Have you ever made a financial decision you later regretted — even though part of you knew better at the time?" Let 2–3 students share briefly. This normalises the topic before the biases are named.

Core teaching point: Behavioural economics is not about being "bad with money." It is about universal, predictable ways human brains process financial decisions poorly under certain conditions — and how to build systems that route around those weaknesses.
B
Bwalya Tembo
24 · Boda boda rider · Ndola
Bwalya knew the maths. Sports betting apps take a cut on every bet — over time, the house always wins. He explained this clearly to a friend one afternoon. That same evening, he placed three bets on a football match, certain "tonight felt different."

He lost K180 — nearly a full day's earnings.

Bwalya is not unintelligent. He is human. His brain, like everyone's, is wired with predictable shortcuts that made perfect sense for survival thousands of years ago but actively work against sound financial decisions today. This module names those shortcuts — so you can recognise them in yourself before they cost you.
💡 Why This MattersYou cannot out-willpower a cognitive bias you don't know exists. Naming these patterns is the first step. Building systems that route around them — not relying on willpower alone — is the second, and far more powerful, step.

Core framework
Core Framework

Six Traps Your Brain Sets for Your Wallet

These are not character flaws. They are well-documented, universal patterns in how humans process financial risk and reward.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Teaching the 6 Traps (10 min)
For each trap, ask: "Has this happened to you or someone you know?" Real examples land far better than definitions alone.

Loss Aversion is the most powerful of the six — Kahneman & Tversky's original 1979 research found losses feel roughly 2.5× more painful than equivalent gains feel good. This asymmetry drives an enormous amount of poor financial behaviour, from refusing to sell a falling investment to avoiding checking a bank balance during a hard month.
🎲
1. Gambler's Fallacy
Believing a random independent event is "due" to happen because of recent history. Each football match, each bet, each spin is statistically independent of the last — the odds never "remember" your previous losses.
📉
2. Loss Aversion
Losses feel roughly 2.5× more painful than equivalent gains feel good (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). This is why people hold onto a falling investment far too long — selling would make the loss "real."
⚓
3. Sunk Cost Fallacy
Continuing to invest in something because of what you've already spent — money already gone that has zero bearing on whether continuing is a good idea now. Only future costs and returns should matter to your next decision.
⏱️
4. Present Bias
Overweighting immediate rewards against future ones — even when the future reward is mathematically much larger. This is exactly what the K500-vs-K700 choice in Section 1 tested.
🗂️
5. Mental Accounting
Treating money differently depending on its source or intended "category" — spending a bonus more freely than a salary, even though a kwacha is a kwacha regardless of where it came from.
👥
6. Social Proof
Following a financial decision because "everyone is doing it" — the exact mechanism behind investment bubbles, get-rich-quick schemes, and pressure to join unverified chilimba groups.
⚠️ These traps compoundBwalya's story shows Gambler's Fallacy and Loss Aversion working together — each loss made him feel a win was "due," and each new bet was partly an attempt to avoid accepting the previous loss. Recognising one trap often reveals a second sitting right behind it.

Practical application
The Practical Answer

You Cannot Out-Willpower a Cognitive Bias

The traps in Section 2 are hardwired — trying to simply "be more disciplined" rarely works for long. Systems that route around the bias entirely work far better.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Systems Discussion (7 min)
Ask the group: "Which of these three systems could you realistically set up this week?" Push for a specific commitment, not a vague intention.

Connect back to Module 1.1: The automatic savings transfer taught there is itself a system defeating present bias — this module gives students the theory behind why that habit works so well.
🗑️
System 1 — Remove the Option
Delete betting apps entirely rather than relying on willpower each time the urge appears. You cannot act on a temptation that isn't sitting on your home screen.
📝
System 2 — Pre-Commit in Advance
Decide your spending or savings rules before the emotional moment arrives — e.g. an automatic transfer on payday, decided weeks in advance, when your judgment isn't clouded by an in-the-moment urge.
⏳
System 3 — The 24-Hour Rule
For any non-essential purchase above a threshold you set, wait 24 hours before buying. This alone defeats present bias — most impulse urges fade substantially within a day.

Quick Fire — 5 Questions

0/5
1
Gambler's Fallacy
After losing 5 bets in a row on independent, random events, is a win "due"?
❌ Not for independent events. Each bet has the exact same odds regardless of what happened before.
✅ Correct! This is the Gambler's Fallacy. Independent random events have no memory of previous outcomes.
2
Loss Aversion
According to Kahneman & Tversky's research, how much more painful do losses feel compared to equivalent gains?
❌ Not quite — losses are felt significantly more strongly than equivalent gains.
✅ Correct! This asymmetry is why people hold falling investments far too long.
3
Sunk Cost Fallacy
You've already spent K3,000 renovating a shop that isn't profitable. Should that K3,000 affect your decision to continue?
❌ This is the Sunk Cost Fallacy. Past spending has no bearing on whether continuing makes sense now.
✅ Correct! The K3,000 is gone either way. Only the decision going forward matters.
4
Systems vs Willpower
What is the most reliable way to defeat a cognitive bias like present bias?
❌ Willpower in the moment is unreliable — the bias is strongest exactly when you're deciding.
✅ Correct! Pre-commitment (like an automatic savings transfer) defeats the bias structurally, not through willpower.
5
Mental Accounting
You spend a K1,000 bonus more freely than K1,000 of your salary. What is this called?
❌ Social Proof is about following others' financial decisions — not this.
✅ Correct! Treating money differently based on its source, even though a kwacha is a kwacha regardless of origin.
