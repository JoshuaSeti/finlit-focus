Introduction
Why This Matters

Every financial decision you make involves these concepts — whether you know them or not.

When Zambia's inflation peaked at 24.6% in 2021 and stayed high through 2024, many Zambians felt it in their grocery bills — but couldn't explain what was happening or why. Now, in mid-2026, inflation has fallen to 6.5% — inside the Bank of Zambia's target band for the first time in years. Understanding what caused the rise, and what brought it down, is one of the most important things a Zambian can know about money. This module explains all of it.

👩🏾
Mwamba Kasonde
32 years old · Teacher · Ndola
Mwamba earns K6,800 per month. In 2020, that salary felt comfortable — she could save K800 per month after expenses.

By 2024, her salary had been raised to K7,500. She should be better off. But she felt poorer. Her savings had dropped to K200 per month. Her grocery bill alone had gone from K1,800 to K2,700.

"My salary went up — so why does it feel like I'm earning less?"

The answer is one word: inflation. And understanding it changes how you think about every financial decision you make.

Money, Prices & Interest
Concepts 1 – 5

Inflation, Purchasing Power, Interest Rates & Compound Interest

Inflation and purchasing power are two sides of the same coin. When inflation goes up, purchasing power goes down. Together they explain why Mwamba's salary increase felt like a pay cut — and why interest rates matter so much to what you save and borrow.

📈
Inflation
in · flay · shun
The rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises over time, causing each unit of currency to buy less than it did before.
In Zambia, inflation is measured by the Central Statistical Office (CSO) using a basket of goods and services that a typical Zambian household buys — including food, transport, housing, clothing, and utilities. This is called the Consumer Price Index (CPI). When the CPI rises, inflation is positive. When prices fall, it is called deflation — which is rare.
Inflation Rate = ((CPI This Year − CPI Last Year) ÷ CPI Last Year) × 100
🇿🇲 Zambian ExampleIn January 2020, a bag of mealie meal cost K90. By January 2024, the same bag cost K160. That is a price increase of 78% over four years — driven by inflation. Zambia's annual inflation peaked at 24.6% in July 2021, remained elevated through 2022–2024, then fell sharply. By June 2026, annual inflation was recorded at 6.5% by the Zambia Statistics Agency — the lowest since February 2018 and now inside the Bank of Zambia's target band of 6–8%. The dramatic fall was driven by a bumper maize harvest, lower fuel prices, and a strengthening kwacha.

💡 What drives inflation in Zambia?Four main causes: (1) Fuel prices — Zambia imports most of its fuel, so global oil price rises pass through immediately. (2) Kwacha weakness — when the kwacha depreciates, imported goods cost more kwacha. (3) Electricity shortages — load-shedding raises production costs for manufacturers. (4) Money supply — when government borrows heavily and new money enters the economy, prices rise.
💸
Purchasing Power
pur · cha · sing pow · er
The amount of goods and services that one unit of currency can buy. When inflation rises, purchasing power falls — the same amount of money buys less.
Mwamba's salary went from K6,800 in 2020 to K7,500 in 2024 — a 10.3% nominal increase. But inflation over the same period was approximately 65% cumulatively. Her real purchasing power fell significantly.
Real Purchasing Power = Nominal Income ÷ Price Level (CPI)
🇿🇲 The Real NumbersK6,800 in 2020 could buy approximately 75 bags of mealie meal (at K90 each). K7,500 in 2024 can buy approximately 47 bags of mealie meal (at K160 each). Her salary in kwacha went up. Her salary in real goods went down by 37%.
🔢
Real vs Nominal
ree · ul vs nom · i · nul
Nominal value is the face value of money — the number on the banknote or salary slip. Real value adjusts for inflation — what that number actually buys in the real world.
When a politician says "government spending increased by 20%," that is a nominal figure. If inflation was 15%, real spending only grew by about 5%. Always ask: is this figure adjusted for inflation?
Real Value = Nominal Value ÷ (1 + Inflation Rate)
🇿🇲 Simple TestYour bank offers a savings account at 8% interest per year. Zambia's inflation rate is 13%. Your nominal return is 8% — your money grew. But your real return is 8% − 13% = −5%. You are actually losing purchasing power every year, even though the number in your account is growing.
💡 A turning point for Zambian saversWith inflation now at 6.5% (June 2026) and savings accounts paying 5–9%, savers are in a better position than they have been in years — the gap between savings rates and inflation has narrowed significantly. However, this is not guaranteed to last. The best strategy remains investing in assets that consistently outpace inflation over the long run. The goal of investing is to grow your money faster than inflation — whether inflation is 6% or 24%.
🏦
Interest Rate
in · ter · est rayt
The cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it — expressed as a percentage of the principal amount per year.
Interest rates in Zambia work in two directions. When you borrow from a bank (a loan or credit card), the bank charges you interest — this is your cost. When you save with a bank (a savings account or fixed deposit), the bank pays you interest — this is your reward. The rate differs significantly: lending rates in Zambia are typically 25–35% per year. Savings rates are typically 4–9% per year. The difference is the bank's profit margin.
🇿🇲 Example — Loan of K10,000You borrow K10,000 from a Zambian commercial bank at 28% per year for 12 months. At the end of the year, you repay K10,000 + K2,800 interest = K12,800 total. That K2,800 is the cost of using someone else's money for one year.
🏛
Monetary Policy Rate (MPR)
muh · net · uh · ree pol · uh · see rayt
The interest rate set by the Bank of Zambia at which it lends to commercial banks. It is the foundation of all other interest rates in Zambia's economy.
The Bank of Zambia (BoZ) uses the MPR as its main tool to control inflation and stabilise the economy. When inflation is too high, BoZ raises the MPR — making borrowing more expensive, slowing spending, and reducing upward pressure on prices. When the economy is slow, BoZ may lower the MPR to make credit cheaper and encourage investment. As of May 2026, the MPR stands at 13.25% — having been cut multiple times from its peak of 14.5% in mid-2025 as inflation moderated rapidly.
🇿🇲 How this affects you directlyWhen BoZ raises the MPR, commercial banks raise their lending rates. Your existing variable-rate loan becomes more expensive. Your new home loan costs more. Your business overdraft rate increases. When BoZ cuts the MPR, the reverse happens — borrowing becomes cheaper.

🔁
Compound Interest
kom · pownd in · ter · est
Interest calculated on both the initial principal AND the accumulated interest from previous periods. In other words — interest earning interest. This is the most powerful force in personal finance.
Albert Einstein allegedly called compound interest the "eighth wonder of the world." Whether he said it or not, the math is real. A K10,000 investment earning 20% per year does not simply add K2,000 each year — it earns interest on the growing total. After 10 years, it is worth over K61,000 — not K30,000 as simple interest would give.
Amount = Principal × (1 + Rate)^Years Example: K10,000 × (1 + 0.20)^10 = K61,917
🇿🇲 The Power of Starting EarlyGrace invests K500/month from age 22 at 15% per year. At age 60 she has K15.2 million. Bwalya waits until age 32 — also K500/month at 15%. At age 60 he has K3.4 million. Same investment amount. 10 fewer years. Grace has 4.5x more — because compound interest had more time to work.
⚠️ Compound interest works against you tooOn a loan at 28% per year, if you miss payments and interest compounds, your debt grows at the same exponential rate. A K5,000 unpaid loan at 28% compounding monthly becomes K6,571 after one year if you make no payments. After two years: K8,618. The same force that builds wealth can trap you in debt.

Investment Concepts
Concepts 6 – 9

Risk, Return, Liquidity, Diversification & Opportunity Cost

🎲
Risk vs Return
risk vs re · turn
The principle that higher potential returns on an investment are always accompanied by higher risk of loss. There is no investment that offers high returns with guaranteed safety — that combination does not exist.
Every investment in Zambia sits somewhere on the risk-return spectrum. Treasury Bills are issued by the Zambian government — very low risk, currently yielding 10.5–12.5% per year (as of May 2026 BoZ auction). LuSE shares carry more risk — prices can fall — but the LuSE All Share Index has grown approximately 20% per year on average from 2018 to 2024. A friend's business — potentially high return, but extremely high risk of total loss.
🇿🇲 Risk Spectrum — Zambia
💰 Bank savings account (4–9%)Lowest risk
📜 Government Treasury Bills (10.5–12.5%)Very low risk
📊 LuSE shares (varies widely)Medium risk
🏗 Property investment (illiquid)Medium-high risk
🚀 Crypto / unlisted startupsVery high risk
🚨 If it sounds too good to be trueAny investment promising guaranteed returns well above current market rates with zero risk is a scam. In Zambia's current environment, government Treasury Bills — the lowest-risk investment available — yield 10.5–12.5% per year. Any offer promising 30%, 50%, or 100%+ with "guaranteed safety" is not a legitimate investment. There is no legitimate investment that offers dramatic above-market returns with zero risk.
💧
Liquidity
li · kwid · i · tee
How quickly and easily an asset can be converted into cash without significantly affecting its price. Cash is perfectly liquid. Property is highly illiquid.
Many Zambians invest heavily in land and property. These assets can hold value and appreciate well — but they are illiquid. If you need K50,000 urgently, you cannot sell half a plot. You either sell the whole thing (possibly below market value because you're rushed) or you borrow against it. Liquidity mismatch is when your assets are long-term but your needs are short-term.
🇿🇲 Liquidity Spectrum
Fully liquid: Cash, MoMo wallet, bank current account — available instantly
Very liquid: Treasury Bills — can be sold before maturity in secondary market
Moderately liquid: LuSE shares — can sell in days if there is a buyer
Illiquid: Property, vehicles, business equipment — days, weeks, or months to sell
🥗
Diversification
di · ver · si · fi · kay · shun
Spreading investments across different assets, sectors, or asset classes so that poor performance in one area does not destroy your entire portfolio. Called "the only free lunch in investing."
A common mistake among Zambian savers is putting everything into one thing — often a single piece of land or a single business. If that investment fails, everything is lost. A diversified Zambian portfolio might include: savings account (emergency fund), Treasury Bills (stable income), LuSE shares (growth), and one property (long-term appreciation). Each behaves differently in different economic conditions.
🇿🇲 The Chilimba TrapA chilimba (informal savings group) can be a form of forced saving — useful. But some Zambians put all their savings into chilimba. If the organiser disappears, everything is lost. Chilimba as one component of a savings plan: smart. Chilimba as your only savings plan: a concentrated risk.
🚪
Opportunity Cost
op · uh · choo · ni · tee kost
The value of the next best alternative you give up when making a choice. Every financial decision has a hidden cost — what you could have done instead with the same money.
You keep K20,000 under your mattress for a year. You feel safe. But the opportunity cost is approximately K2,500 in T-Bill returns you did not earn (at 12.5%), or K1,400 in savings account interest (at 7%). The cash was not just sitting there — it was silently costing you money. And with inflation currently at 6.5%, the real value of that K20,000 fell by K1,300 over the year.
🇿🇲 Every Choice Has a CostBuying a new K80,000 car: opportunity cost = K80,000 invested in T-Bills for 3 years at 12.5% ≈ K114,000 by year three. Spending K500 per month on eating out: opportunity cost = K500/month invested for 10 years at 12% ≈ K114,000. The question is never just "can I afford this?" — it is also "what am I giving up?"

Economy & Currency
Concepts 10 – 14

Supply & Demand, Budget Deficit, GDP, Exchange Rates & The Kwacha

📊
Supply & Demand
suh · ply and di · mand
The relationship between how much of something is available (supply) and how much people want it (demand). When demand exceeds supply, prices rise. When supply exceeds demand, prices fall.
This single principle explains almost every price change you see in Zambia. During load-shedding, Lusaka's tomato prices double — not because farmers decided to be greedy, but because electricity shortages disrupted cold storage and transport, reducing supply. During harvest season, the same tomatoes cost a quarter of the price — because supply suddenly floods the market. The price you see at the market is supply and demand in action every single day.
🇿🇲 Three Zambian Examples
Mealie meal shortage (2021–22): Poor harvests reduced supply. Demand stayed the same. Price rose from K90 to K150+.
LuSE shares: When more people want to buy a share than sell it, the price rises — pure demand. When holders rush to sell (a market panic), supply exceeds demand and prices fall.
Rental property in Lusaka: Growing urban population (demand) + limited new housing (supply) = rising rents year after year.
🏛
Budget Deficit
buj · et def · i · sit
When a government spends more money than it collects in taxes and other revenue in a given year. The difference must be borrowed — adding to the national debt.
Zambia experienced significant budget deficits throughout the 2010s, borrowing heavily from international markets. By 2020, Zambia became the first African country to default on its sovereign bonds during COVID-19 — unable to make payments on its external debt. This directly affected Zambians: the kwacha weakened dramatically, donor funding froze, and the government had less money for roads, hospitals, and schools. Under President Hichilema's government, Zambia reached a debt restructuring agreement in 2023 — a critical step toward fiscal stability.
Budget Deficit = Government Spending − Government Revenue (taxes + other income)
🇿🇲 Why this affects your daily lifeGovernment deficits affect you in three ways: (1) Borrowing crowds out private credit — when government borrows heavily, banks have less to lend to businesses, pushing up commercial interest rates. (2) Kwacha weakness — debt repayments in foreign currency drain reserves, weakening the kwacha. (3) Inflation risk — if the central bank prints money to cover deficits, inflation rises.
📉
GDP — Gross Domestic Product
jee · dee · pee
The total monetary value of all goods and services produced within a country in a given year. It is the most widely used measure of an economy's size and health.
Zambia's GDP was approximately USD 28 billion in 2024. When GDP grows, the economy is expanding — more jobs, more income, more investment. When GDP shrinks for two consecutive quarters, it is called a recession. GDP per capita (GDP divided by population) gives a rough sense of average income — Zambia's is around USD 1,300 per person per year, reflecting the wide inequality between urban formal workers and rural informal workers.
🇿🇲 What drives Zambia's GDP?Zambia's economy is heavily driven by copper mining (Zambia is the world's 7th largest copper producer), agriculture (maize, tobacco, soya), tourism, and a growing services sector. When global copper prices fall, Zambia's entire economy feels it — revenue drops, the kwacha weakens, and government has less to spend.
💱
Exchange Rate
ex · chanj rayt
The price at which one country's currency can be exchanged for another. It determines the cost of imported goods, the value of foreign earnings, and the burden of foreign debt.
The kwacha's exchange rate against the US dollar is the most watched financial figure in Zambia. As of July 2026, the exchange rate is approximately K18 per USD — a remarkable strengthening from the K28 peak seen in early 2025. The kwacha has appreciated nearly 20% in 2026, driven by rising copper export earnings, improved investor confidence following Zambia's debt restructuring, and lower import demand. In 2012, the rate was K5 per USD, and the long years of depreciation to K28 meant everything Zambia imported cost far more kwacha. The recent strengthening is a significant positive development.
If USD/ZMW = 18: K18,000 buys USD 1,000. If kwacha weakens to K22: K22,000 needed for same USD 1,000 — K4,000 more. If kwacha strengthens to K15: only K15,000 needed — K3,000 saved.
🇿🇲 What Moves the Kwacha?
Copper prices: Higher copper prices = more USD flowing into Zambia = stronger kwacha.
Fuel imports: Zambia spends large amounts of USD on fuel — increasing demand for dollars, weakening the kwacha.
Government borrowing: Debt repayments in USD reduce reserves, weakening the kwacha.
Investor confidence: When investors trust Zambia's economic management, they buy kwacha assets — strengthening it.

💡 The kwacha in 2026 — a different storyThe kwacha has strengthened nearly 20% in 2026, supported by copper mining inflows and Zambia's completed debt restructuring. This is genuinely good news — imports are cheaper, inflation has fallen, and the economy is stabilising. However, kwacha strength is not guaranteed to continue. Long-term financial planning should account for exchange rate volatility in both directions. (1) Maintain some USD-linked exposure as a hedge — REIZ REIT on the LuSE is USD-denominated. (2) A stronger kwacha makes imported goods cheaper — this is one reason inflation has fallen so fast. (3) T-Bill yields have also fallen with inflation — currently 10.5–12.5% — but still offer real positive returns above current inflation of 6.5%.
🏪
Formal vs Informal Economy
for · mul vs in · for · mul ee · kon · uh · mee
The formal economy includes registered businesses, employed workers, and activities recorded in official statistics. The informal economy includes unregistered traders, casual workers, and activities that go largely unrecorded — but are no less real.
Zambia's informal economy is estimated to account for 50–70% of total economic activity — one of the highest proportions in the world. The traders in Soweto Market, the minibus drivers, the roadside vegetable sellers, the tailors operating from home — all are part of the informal economy. They pay little or no tax, have no employment contracts, and often lack access to formal banking. This creates a challenge: government collects less tax revenue, and these workers have no social protection. FinLit Zambia's mission directly addresses this gap — financial literacy enables informal workers to make better financial decisions and transition toward formal financial tools.

Quick Fire — 6 Questions

0/6
1
Inflation & Purchasing Power
Mwamba's salary increased by 10% this year. Zambia's inflation rate was 14%. What happened to her real purchasing power?
❌ The nominal salary went up, but we need to account for inflation.
❌ Not quite — the raise didn't fully offset the inflation rate.
✅ Correct. When your nominal income grows slower than inflation, your real purchasing power falls. Mwamba's real wages declined by approximately 4% — she can buy less this year than last year, despite the raise.
2
Compound Interest
You invest K10,000 at 20% per year for 3 years, with compound interest. How much do you have at the end?
❌ That would be simple interest — K10,000 + (K2,000 × 3) = K16,000. But compound interest grows faster.
✅ Correct. Year 1: K10,000 × 1.2 = K12,000. Year 2: K12,000 × 1.2 = K14,400. Year 3: K14,400 × 1.2 = K17,280. The extra K1,280 over simple interest is compound interest at work.
❌ Not quite — at 20% compounding, it takes about 3.8 years to double (the Rule of 72: 72 ÷ 20 = 3.6 years).
3
Liquidity
Grace owns a plot in Makeni worth K200,000. She urgently needs K50,000 for a medical emergency. What is her problem?
❌ The value is there, but the issue is access to that value quickly.
✅ Correct. She is "land-rich, cash-poor." She cannot sell half a plot, and selling the whole plot at fair value takes weeks or months. This is a classic liquidity mismatch — long-term asset, short-term urgent need. Lesson: always maintain a liquid emergency fund separate from long-term investments.
❌ Supply and demand affects the price, but the core issue here is liquidity.
4
The MPR
The Bank of Zambia raises the Monetary Policy Rate from 12.5% to 14%. What is the most likely direct effect on you?
❌ When MPR rises, savings rates typically rise too — banks need to attract deposits to lend out.
✅ Correct. Commercial banks use the MPR as their base cost of funds. When it rises, they pass the increase on to borrowers. Your existing variable-rate loan becomes more expensive. New loans cost more. This is how BoZ slows inflation — by making borrowing and spending more expensive.
❌ Actually, raising interest rates often strengthens a currency by attracting foreign capital seeking higher yields. But this isn't the most direct effect on an individual.
5
Risk vs Return
A stranger approaches you with an investment opportunity: "Guaranteed 80% return in 3 months, completely safe." What should you do?
❌ The risk here is not worth taking, and the "guaranteed" claim is the problem.
❌ No amount of additional detail makes this offer legitimate. The structure of the claim itself is the red flag.
✅ Correct. The fundamental principle of risk vs return means high returns always carry high risk. When someone promises guaranteed returns dramatically above market rates (Zambia's T-Bills currently yield 10.5–12.5% per year — any offer promising 80% in 3 months is a scam). Every time. Walk away.
6
Opportunity Cost
You keep K15,000 in cash at home for a year "for safety." Zambia's 364-day Treasury Bill rate is 12.5%. What is the opportunity cost of this decision?
❌ Cash has no explicit cost, but there is always an opportunity cost — what you could have earned instead.
❌ Inflation is part of the real cost, but the opportunity cost is specifically about the alternative investment foregone.
✅ Correct. K15,000 × 12.5% = K1,875 in T-Bill returns you gave up by holding cash. That is the opportunity cost. Plus, with inflation at 6.5%, the real value of that cash fell by K975. Total real cost of the "safe" cash decision: approximately K2,850 in one year — not nothing.
Key Terms
Inflation
The rate at which prices rise over time, so each kwacha buys less.
Purchasing Power
What your money can actually buy. It falls when inflation rises.
Real vs Nominal
Nominal is the face value. Real is the value after adjusting for inflation.
Interest Rate
The cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it, as a percentage per year.
MPR
Monetary Policy Rate. The Bank of Zambia's rate, which sets the base for all other interest rates.
Compound Interest
Interest earned on both your original money and the interest already added.
Risk vs Return
Higher possible returns always come with a higher risk of loss.
Liquidity
How quickly an asset can be turned into cash without losing value.
Diversification
Spreading money across different assets so one failure does not wipe you out.
Opportunity Cost
The value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice.
Supply & Demand
When demand beats supply prices rise. When supply beats demand prices fall.
Budget Deficit
When government spends more than it collects in revenue in a year.
GDP
The total value of goods and services a country produces in a year.
Exchange Rate
The price of one currency in another, such as kwacha per US dollar.
