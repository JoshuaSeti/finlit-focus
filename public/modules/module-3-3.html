Core Concept 1

What the Banking and Financial Services Act, 2026 Actually Changed

This law replaced the old 2017 Act and the Money-lenders Act entirely — and it's the strongest consumer protection framework Zambia has had.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — The 2026 Act (7 min)
The single most practically useful fact in this section: microfinance and digital lending apps are now required to be licensed under the same BoZ framework as banks. An app with no visible licence information is now operating illegally, not just informally.

Mandatory credit-data sharing is worth connecting explicitly to Module 1.2 — this is the legal backbone making the CRB system work.
🚫
No More Unwarranted Fees
Financial institutions must get Bank of Zambia approval before introducing or raising any consumer charge. A fee you were never told about, added after the fact, violates this directly.
📵
No Harassment or Deception
The Act explicitly prohibits harassment or abusive debt collection practices, and deceptive or misleading conduct toward consumers.
📜
Universal Licensing
A single-window licensing regime now covers banks, microfinance institutions, and digital lenders alike. An unlicensed lender — however established it looks — is operating outside the law.
📊
Mandatory Credit-Data Sharing
Lenders are now required to share credit data with reference bureaus. This is the legal foundation behind the TransUnion CRB system covered in Module 1.2 — not optional, and not a privacy violation.
⚠️ An honest limitation, worth knowingA World Bank diagnostic review found Zambia has had as few as 5 officers nationally responsible for financial consumer protection. Enforcement capacity is genuinely limited — which makes documenting your own complaint clearly, and escalating correctly, more important, not less.

Core Concept 2

The Real Complaint Process — Step by Step

Knowing your rights only matters if you know the actual, realistic sequence for using them.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — The Complaint Process (6 min)
Be honest about step 3. Zambia does not yet have a dedicated financial services ombudsman — it's currently under consideration, not yet built. This means the regulator route, while slower than an ombudsman would be, is the real current option. Courts exist as a last resort but can take years — this is realistic, not the process being taught as ideal.
1
Complain to the institution directly, in writing
Every institution has an internal complaints process. Keep a written record — email or a dated letter, not just a phone call.
2
Give them a reasonable window to respond
Document the date you complained and what response (if any) you received.
3
Escalate to the correct regulator
Bank of Zambia for banks and lenders · PIA for insurance · SEC for investment schemes (from Module 0.6). No dedicated ombudsman exists yet in Zambia — the sector regulator is the real current escalation point.
4
Courts — genuinely a last resort
Available, but realistically slow — a World Bank review noted this route can take years. Worth knowing it exists, not worth expecting a fast outcome from it.
📌 Why documentation matters more here than almost anywhere elseWith as few as 5 national officers handling financial consumer protection, a clearly documented complaint — dates, amounts, written correspondence — is genuinely more likely to get a result than a verbal complaint alone. This isn't about distrust; it's about being realistic about a stretched system.

Key Terms
Banking and Financial Services Act, 2026
Replaced the 2017 Act and the Money-lenders Act. Expanded BoZ oversight and consumer protection.
Single-Window Licensing
One unified licensing regime now covering banks, microfinance, and digital lenders.
Unwarranted Fee
Any consumer charge introduced or raised without prior Bank of Zambia approval.
Financial Services Ombudsman
A proposed but not-yet-established independent dispute resolution body for Zambia.
Mandatory Credit-Data Sharing
Lenders' legal obligation to report to credit reference bureaus like TransUnion.
