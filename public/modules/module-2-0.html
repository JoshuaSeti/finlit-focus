Opening decision
The Opening Problem

One choice, made six months before an accident, decided whether Milimo paid K500 or K9,000.

Insurance is the only financial product you buy hoping never to use it. That makes it easy to underrate — until the moment it either saves you or doesn't. Walk through Milimo's story below and choose his insurance path yourself.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Opening (4 min)
Opening question: "Has anyone ever skipped insurance to save money — car, phone, health — and had it work out fine? Has anyone regretted it?" Both answers are common; that unpredictability is exactly why insurance exists.

Key teaching point: Insurance isn't about avoiding all risk — it's about converting a rare, catastrophic cost into a small, predictable one. Let all three of Milimo's paths play out before revealing which is "best" — the point is to feel the trade-off, not be told the answer first.

Milimo's Decision
Same accident. Different choices made months earlier. Walk through Milimo's story and see how it could have gone.
Six Months Before
Milimo has just bought a used car worth K35,000. A broker quotes him third-party insurance at K900/year, or comprehensive cover at K2,400/year. Money is tight this month.
Six Months Later
At an intersection in Lusaka, another driver runs a red light and hits Milimo's car. The other driver is clearly at fault. Milimo's car needs K9,000 in repairs. The other driver has no insurance and no way to pay.
💥 No Insurance
With no cover of any kind, Milimo has no claim to file. The other driver is at fault but uninsured and has no assets worth pursuing legally. Milimo must pay the full repair cost himself — from savings meant for something else entirely.
Out of pocket
− K 9,000
⚖️ Third-Party Only
Third-party insurance covers damage Milimo causes to others — not damage to his own vehicle. Since the other driver was at fault and uninsured, Milimo's policy offers no help here. He still pays for his own repairs, though at least he avoided any legal penalty for driving uninsured.
Out of pocket
− K 9,000
🛡️ Comprehensive Cover
Comprehensive insurance covers damage to Milimo's own car regardless of who was at fault. His insurer pays for the repair directly (minus a small excess) and then pursues the other driver separately — that's the insurer's problem to chase, not Milimo's.
Out of pocket (excess only)
− K 500

Is Insurance Worth It? Run the Real Numbers

Insurance is a bet — you're betting the premium against the probability and size of a loss. This calculator makes that bet visible.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Cost-Benefit (6 min)
Walk through one example first: Asset value K35,000 (Milimo's car), premium K2,400/year, risk probability 15%. Expected loss = K35,000 × 15% = K5,250 — well above the K2,400 premium, so insurance makes clear mathematical sense here.

Key nuance: even when "expected value" favours skipping insurance, most people still buy it — because a rare catastrophic loss (losing K35,000 at once) hurts far more than paying small premiums steadily. This is loss aversion from Module 3.1, applied usefully here.

Core Concept 2

The Premium-vs-Excess Trade-Off

A lower premium usually means a higher excess — the amount you pay yourself before your insurer covers the rest. Neither choice is automatically right; it depends on how often you expect to claim.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Premium vs Excess (6 min)
The core insight: a high-excess, low-premium policy rewards people who rarely claim. A low-excess, high-premium policy rewards people who claim often or want cost certainty. Neither is "better" — it depends on your own claim frequency and risk tolerance.

Connect to Milimo's story: his comprehensive policy's K500 excess is exactly this trade-off in action — he accepted a higher premium for a lower excess, and it paid off.
💰
Premium
The amount you pay regularly (usually annually) to keep your policy active — whether or not you ever claim.
🧾
Excess
The amount you pay yourself toward any claim before your insurer covers the remainder. Higher excess usually means a lower premium.

Core Concept 3

What Insurance Actually Exists in Zambia

Beyond car insurance, several types of cover matter for different life stages — some legally required, some optional but valuable.

👩‍🏫 Facilitator Note — Types of Insurance (5 min)
Connect to earlier modules: NHIMA (Module 0.6/1.0) is Zambia's mandatory national health scheme — a form of health insurance already covered elsewhere. This section focuses on the insurance types not yet discussed: motor, life, and property.

Regulator reminder: the Pensions and Insurance Authority (PIA) — introduced in Module 0.6 — is the correct place to direct any insurance complaint or dispute.
🚗
Motor Third-Party Insurance — Legally Mandatory
Every vehicle on Zambian roads must carry at least third-party insurance, covering damage you cause to others. Driving without it is a legal offence, separate from any financial risk.
🛡️
Motor Comprehensive Insurance — Optional but Valuable
Covers damage to your own vehicle regardless of fault, as Milimo's story showed. Not legally required, but the only option that protects your own asset.
👪
Life Insurance
Pays a lump sum to named beneficiaries if you die during the policy term. Especially relevant for anyone with dependents relying on their income — connects directly to the beneficiary-naming habit from Module 4.0.
🏠
Property / Home Insurance
Covers damage to a home or its contents from fire, theft, or weather events. Relevant once you own or are renting a home with valuable contents.
🏥
NHIMA — Already Covered
Zambia's mandatory national health insurance scheme, funded by the 1% employee + 1% employer contribution discussed in Module 1.0. Covers a defined package of health services.
📌 Insurance Priority Order for a Young Zambian Earner1. Motor third-party (if you drive — legally required). 2. NHIMA (automatic if formally employed). 3. Emergency fund first (Module 1.3) before adding more insurance. 4. Life insurance once you have dependents relying on your income. 5. Comprehensive motor or property cover once you own an asset worth protecting.

An asset is worth K20,000. The annual premium is K800. The probability of loss this year is 3%. What is the expected loss?
❌ That's the premium, not the expected loss calculation.
✅ Correct! Expected loss = Asset Value × Probability = K20,000 × 0.03 = K600. Since this is below the K800 premium, the insurer is pricing in a margin — normal and expected.
❌ That would be the loss if it definitely happened — expected loss weights it by probability.
❌ The percentage must be applied to the asset value, not used alone.

Key Terms
Premium
The regular amount paid to keep an insurance policy active.
Excess
The amount you pay yourself before the insurer covers the rest of a claim.
Third-Party Insurance
Legally mandatory. Covers damage you cause to others, not your own vehicle.
Comprehensive Insurance
Optional. Covers your own vehicle regardless of fault.
Expected Loss
Asset Value × Probability of Loss. Used to judge if a premium is worth paying.
PIA
Pensions and Insurance Authority. Zambia's insurance and pensions regulator.
Life Insurance
Pays a lump sum to named beneficiaries upon the policyholder's death.
NHIMA
Zambia's mandatory national health insurance scheme (covered in Module 1.0).
